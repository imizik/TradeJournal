## Morning report

**What I investigated.** Would you have kept more money if each live position
alert had simply closed the position? The study replays every past trade's
option minute bars, sells at the next minute's open (less a 3% spread
haircut) when an alert's condition is met, and compares that with what you
actually did. It also compares each alert's moment with a random minute of the
same trade.

**What the evidence showed.** None of the three alerts works as an automatic
exit. All three are **rejected** as a demonstrated saving under the
registered criteria:

| Alert, as a mechanical exit | Trades it touched | Money vs. what you did (98.3% range) | Same trades, random minute | Return on premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Give-back (+10% then back to +3%) | 206 | **+$2,891** (−$14,612 to +$21,814) | −$990 | −2.4 points |
| Held an hour | 337 | **−$10,170** (−$28,843 to +$7,828) | −$10,305 | −1.4 points |
| Expiry day, 14:30 | 38 | **−$2,848** (−$7,771 to +$594) | −$325 | −8.1 points |

The pattern is the same for all three:

- **Each alert saves money on the trades that ended as losers, and gives it
  back on the trades that recovered.**
  - Give-back: +$28,014 on 126 eventual losers, −$25,122 on 80 eventual
    winners.
  - Held an hour: +$25,710 on losers, −$35,880 on winners.
  - Expiry day: +$869 on 31 losers, −$3,717 on 7 winners.
- **The typical trade comes out slightly better under every rule** (median
  +$22, +$10 and +$4). The few big recoveries outweigh that.
- **The audit only saw the losing side.** "53 losers had been up 10%" was
  true. It did not count the trades that dipped just as far and came back.

Details:

- **Give-back** is a coin flip that rests on one trade. Without its best case
  (+$4,347) it is −$1,456. At the stress cost (9% spread) it is −$4,711. The
  first half lost (−$3,138) and the second half gained (+$6,030). Only in the
  second half did its moment beat a random minute with an interval above zero.
  That is one period, and it is not the registered test.
- **Held an hour** would have cost about $10k. It does no better than a random
  minute (timing value +$210). The hour mark carries no information about
  what happens next; the cost is simply the price of exiting early.
- **Expiry day**: the 14:30 exit did worse than a random minute of the same
  trades (−$2,523, with the whole interval below zero). The trades still open
  that late included ones that finished well.

**What this means for you.**

- The alerts are reminders to decide, not sell signals. On your history,
  closing whenever one fired would not have made money, and closing at the
  hour mark would probably have cost money.
- If they help, it will be because you sell the trades that are going wrong
  and keep the ones that are working. Only your future decisions can show
  that.
- Nothing here argues for switching an alert off. They were never meant to be
  automatic exits, and the study cannot test their real job: a prompt that
  makes you look.

**What was rejected:**
- All three alerts as mechanical exit rules.
- Peer-confirmed recovery as a Factory candidate this cycle (data does not
  cover the screen period; the Factory is paused).

**What still looks interesting.**
- **Trades held longer than 10 days** (22 trades, −$5,209) were outside this
  study and are the clearest untested leak.
- **The give-back moment** beat random exits in the April–October half. That
  is worth watching prospectively, not acting on.

**What data would most improve this.** Historical option bid/ask. The
give-back result flips sign between a 3% and a 9% spread, so the guessed
spread decides it.

**The single best next experiment.** Score your decisions after each live
alert. For every alert from October 21 onward, compare what you did with the
mechanical exit and with holding. Use the same frozen code and the
exit-habits card's records. The question to answer: when you act on an alert,
do you sell the losers and keep the winners better than the rule does? The
first review comes at 30 give-back alerts or January 29, 2027, whichever is
first, with no threshold changes before then.

## Candidate cards

### C1: the live alerts as mechanical exits (run once; rejected)

| | |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Closing the whole position when a live alert's condition is met would have kept more money than your actual exits |
| Why it might work | The audit found losers held and green trades ridden back through entry; an exit at the alert caps those paths |
| Instruments | Your closed and expired long option trades, 2025-07-10 to 2026-10-19: 1,506 analyzed of 1,528 |
| Data | Journal fills (minute timestamps); Alpaca option 1-minute trade bars |
| Rules | Give-back: armed at ≥ +10% and ≥ $50, exit at ≤ +3%. Held an hour: exit 60 regular-session minutes after first entry, entry day only. Expiry day: exit at 14:30 ET (11:30 on half days) on expiration |
| Execution | Decide at a bar's close, sell at the next bar's open within 5 minutes, otherwise the actual exit stands |
| Costs | 3% of price off the sell (minimum $0.01 a share); stress 9% |
| Results | The table above. Every 98.3% range includes zero; give-back and held-an-hour disagree in sign or fail at the stress cost |
| Benchmarks | Actual exits; 200 random-minute exits per touched trade |
| Validation status | Exploratory (thresholds came from an audit that read both halves) |
| Why results could mislead | Trade prints, not midpoints, so the spread is guessed and decides the give-back sign. A mechanical exit is not what an alert does. Dollars are driven by size. One trade carries the give-back total |
| Decision | **Reject** all three as exit rules. Keep the frozen code for the prospective comparison of your decisions after alerts |

## What the eventual unattended runner needs

For Codex's overnight infrastructure. This lane writes no scheduler, job or
ledger of its own.

1. **Permission:** one read-only extract per run:
   `python -m scripts.exit_counterfactual_study extract --ssh ubuntu@<vps> --out <private dir>/trades.json`.
   It runs `BEGIN READ ONLY; COPY (...) TO STDOUT` as `postgres` and selects
   only trade, fill, tradefill and account type. Better still: run it on the
   VPS as the `tj_app` role, or through a read-only endpoint. The output is
   personal financial data; keep it out of git and artifacts.
2. **Market data:** the Alpaca option-bar cache (read), plus `--fetch` with the
   existing Alpaca keys for missing windows. Market data only, no account or
   order endpoints.
3. **Run:** `python -m scripts.exit_counterfactual_study run --trades … --cache backend/data/alpaca_cache --out <dir> [--fetch]`.
   It is deterministic (seed 20261024) and writes `results.json` (aggregates)
   and `trades.jsonl` (per trade, personal).
4. **Stop conditions:** one historical run per protocol id. The prospective
   review happens once, at 30 give-back alerts or 2027-01-29. A changed
   constant is a new protocol id, recorded alongside this one, never an
   overwrite.
5. **Output for the morning:** `results.json` → this report's format: the
   decision per rule, the dollar difference with its interval, the random-exit
   comparison, and the exclusion counts.
6. **Receipt fields:** `results.json` → `provenance` names the run's inputs
   for a coordinator's receipt: `protocol_id`, `extract_sha256` (the exact
   extract bytes), `source_sha` and `source_dirty` (the checkout the code ran
   from). The recorded 2026-10-24 run's block was added afterwards from the
   same extract and code, without a rerun.
